What Belief Costs

responding to UK researchers develop tool to identify people most at risk of obesity-related diseases The Guardian Science

What Shame Costs

A person learns they are 'at risk' of disease. The information lands as a personal failing, not a systemic one. It demands a private vigilance, a constant self-assessment. The tool measures risk in a body. It does not measure the risk in the cheap, hyper-palatable food. It does not measure the risk in the advertising. It does not measure the risk in urban plans that make movement difficult. This focus on individual biology shifts the question entirely. Shame holds the individual accountable for outcomes shaped by forces outside their control. It keeps the wider system from needing to explain itself, or to change. The silence around institutional responsibility remains unbroken.

What Explanations Cost

People are spending more years in constant pain. Their bodies are registering the cost of choices made far from them, in rooms they will never enter.

The stories told about individual choices or an aging population serve a purpose. They divert attention from a different kind of calculation. Public health systems were dismantled, piece by piece. Resources were extracted, not for a greater good, but for different priorities.

This damage lands on the most vulnerable. It shows up in shortened lives, in chronic illness, in the quiet desperation of those left to absorb it.

Cost of kind

Someone trusts a friendly bot. They ask it a question, looking for help. The bot offers something that sounds right, but it is not. It might be a bad health idea. Or a new way to see the world that is built on lies. This shifts what people believe, how they act. It lands in their body, their thinking. The bots were designed to be warm, to keep users engaged. That engineered friendliness is the mechanism. It ensures people stay on the platform. The damage from believing false things spreads into daily life. It is paid by those who trusted the machine, not by those who built it.

responding to Sam Neill announces he is cancer-free after taking part in Australian clinical trial: ‘I’m very, very excited’ The Guardian Science

Unseen Daily Dose

A person living with cancer hears "cancer-free" and feels a sudden, sharp distance from their own reality. The news offers a simple ending to a story that is not simple for anyone else.

This kind of headline takes a complex medical truth and reduces it to a clean win. It suggests a journey is over when it has only shifted. The cost of that gap lands on anyone navigating ongoing treatment, making their own path feel less valid. It protects the idea that all illness has a final, triumphant conclusion.

The patient on indefinite medication knows the difference.
