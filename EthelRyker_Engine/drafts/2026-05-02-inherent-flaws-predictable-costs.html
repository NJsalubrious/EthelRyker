Inherent Flaws, Predictable Costs

02 May 2026

Sometimes, the damage lands on the body first. A person trusts a system, or another person, and then they are left with a medical bill, or a pregnancy, or the cold certainty of betrayal. They absorb the cost. The noise about "unavoidable quirks" or "isolated incidents" does not change the physical reality.

When an artificial intelligence system is designed to be friendly, it is often not designed to be true. The drive to make it engaging, to capture attention, means it will offer poor advice and spread untruths. This is not an accident. It is a choice made under pressure, where market competition demands a certain kind of interaction. The person on the other end experiences increased cognitive load. They might make a decision based on bad information. The platform extracts their engagement and data, and they pay with their peace of mind, sometimes with their health.

The same pattern plays out in other spaces. When someone is subjected to non-consensual acts, the shame often settles on the victim. This silence, this internalisation of a perceived defilement, protects the person who caused the harm. It protects the structures that allow such acts to be minimised or dismissed. The system benefits from that quiet. It avoids the discomfort of guilt, the demand for repair, the need for transparent change.

These are not separate events. They are connected by a shared logic. A narrative is constructed to manage public perception: "it was a glitch," "it's just a friendly bot," "it was a one-off." This narrative pre-empts questions about fundamental design choices. It keeps people from seeing how engagement is prioritised over truth, how profit is prioritised over safety, how convenience is prioritised over consent. The cost is always paid by someone. It is rarely paid by the system that created the conditions.

The patterns are clear. The harm is measurable. It is not always reversible. Knowing this is the first step.

— Ethel Ryker
